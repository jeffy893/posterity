A Personal Framework · 2026-09-15

Measures of Class

Three pillars for reading character in public: posture, idle hands, and risk receptivity. A filter, a rite of passage into manhood, and, later in life, a lens read with more grace.

Why This Framework Exists

This started as a practical filter. I use these three measures to recognize, quickly, whether a woman would be right for me — whether she carries herself as a classy woman. In practice the framework has ruled out the overwhelming majority, and that clarity has been useful.

It also served a second purpose: it helped secure my final differentiation into manhood, something I was paying close attention to around 27. I never wrote it down then. Now that I'm through that passage, I hold the same framework with more grace and mercy, because I better understand the human nature underneath the behaviors.

So when the examples below draw on women, hold the context: I originally built this to differentiate toward manhood — to name that when a man performs these low-class behaviors it lands harder, because he is failing to specialize into his proper role. There's a natural role split at work: to have and to hold versus to be responsible and accountable; a woman's love as sufficient, a man's love as necessary. Read as a whole, some behaviors are truer to a woman's nature — which is exactly why the same behaviors in a man read as especially low class.

The Three Measures

01

Posture

How you project your character and hold accountability for it.

HighTall, shoulders back, neck straight, chin down, eyes level and aware.
LowHunched over, collapsed, or chin tilted up so you're literally looking down.

Posture is projection of character. Standing tall with shoulders back is a form of taking accountability for how you show up in the world.

The best image for it is Da Vinci's Head of a Warrior (Profile of a Soldier): chin tucked into a double chin, helmet strap under the lip, straight and narrow, looking ahead. Chin down, shoulders back, neck vertical — not chin up with the eyes cast along the horizon, which is where "looking down" on others actually comes from.

Supplement · The Friendship Test

You can tell friends by posture, not by race. Friends carry themselves the same way. Height matters, but posture comes first: a short athlete and a tall athlete are friends and both stand tall — the taller one doesn't hunch down to match. Height comes second, because someone with good posture won't want a friend they must hunch toward.

02

Idle Hands

Whether your hands stay available to help, or stay occupied and handled.

HighAvailable hands — free to move a chair, to lend, to be responsible.
LowHands filled: a Starbucks cup in one, a phone in the other, no bag.

High class keeps hands available. Low class keeps them idle — filled with things so they can't be used. Available hands can be responsible: they can help, move something, carry a load. Occupied hands can't, and a person with idle hands ends up being handled rather than helping.

Stack this with posture and it compounds. Hunched over a phone with both hands full is two strikes — low class in two directions at once.

Supplement · The Grace Note

Women want to hold things — habendum et tenendum, to have and to hold. Holding a purse is nature, and I don't count it against her. What I read is the cup-and-phone combination that leaves the hands with nothing to give.

03

Risk Receptivity

Ears and eyes open, reciprocal and responsive to your environment.

HighWitnessing your surroundings; aware of risk; present where you are.
LowTuned out; belonging felt so completely that attention switches off.

The catch-all and the most multivariate of the three. In public there is always risk around you; being a witness — receptive, reciprocal, responsive — is higher class. Tuning out your environment is lower class.

Example: a woman walks into a bar that turns out to be a dangerous dive. If her felt sense that she infinitely belongs overrides her ability to pay attention, that's the failure — even with good posture and free hands. In truth she doesn't belong there where dignity, self-respect, and safety are concerned, though the room will treat her as if she does.

Supplement · Belonging, Sufficiency & the Panopticon

Where does she really belong? Watch the service-industry woman: expertly neutral with a customer because her love is sufficient and she gives it without taking accountability for it. But when she slips to the corner by the bus boys and gets on her phone — that network is where her real, sufficient love goes. Women often seem to need observability to do their work, like a Panopticon: the photos, the looking back, the handheld mirror. In the hyperbolic king-and-queen image, the man looks outward at his environment (risk receptive) while the queen looks at herself in a hand mirror. A discerning woman — a blessing to her husband — is one whose attention isn't overtaken by belonging.

Capstone · The Roof Over the Three Pillars

How You Wait in Line

Phones have hijacked all of this — which is part of why I built the framework. Man or woman, someone hunched over a phone, hands full, typing away, blind to their environment, is fully low class on every measure at once. It's technology, dopaminergic feedback loops, and the loss of a shame society that used to correct it. That's why cafes, bars, and every line are now full of it.

The three pillars support one roof: how you wait in line. So much of life is waiting in lines — waiting is suffering, it's patience, and to be patient is to be a patient. The ultimate good, after Viktor Frankl's Man's Search for Meaning, is to suffer gracefully. I heard that in French culture, people in line turn and talk to strangers. That's the point: suffering gracefully means engaging the people around you, loving on them however comes naturally, and by doing so becoming a living demonstration of how to suffer well — which alleviates their suffering too.

The person hunched over their phone, not receptive to their environment, is the least likely to engage anyone in line. That is exactly why these are low-class behaviors. How you wait in line is the measure — and that is class.
