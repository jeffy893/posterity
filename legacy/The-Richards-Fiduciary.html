The Richards Fiduciary

A Biblical Narrative of the Appalachian People
• • •

From the ancient mounds along the Licking River
to the tobacco fields of Bath County,
from the forts of the dark and bloody ground
to the deserts of Yuma,
from the forges of Detroit
to the stars above Pasadena—

This is the record of the house of Richards,
written that their children's children may know
from whence they came, and unto what they are called.

I. Genesis

The Mound Builders and the Land of Tomorrow
"In the beginning was the land, and the land was without name,
and the spirit of the Creator moved upon the waters of the Licking River."

1In the beginning, before the white man set foot upon the dark and fertile soil, the land which would be called Kain-tuck-ee lay in stillness beneath the heavens. And the word Kain-tuck-ee signifieth "the land of tomorrow," for it was a country set apart, a place of promise held in reserve by the hand of Providence.

2And the land was not empty, for from time immemorial the peoples of the earth had dwelt therein. The Adena, who came five hundred years before the birth of Christ, raised great mounds along the ridges and the river bottoms—monuments to their dead, temples to their understanding of the world beyond the veil.

3In Bath County, upon a hill near Sharpsburg, the Adena built the Ramey Mound, an earthen enclosure standing three feet above the valley floor, recorded first by the white man in the year eighteen hundred and seven, though it had stood for two thousand years before that reckoning.

4And in the year nineteen hundred and thirty-two, men of learning surveyed the county and found therein fourteen ancient sites—burial places near the Springfield Presbyterian Church, and mounds scattered across the hills like altars left by a forgotten congregation. And within these giant mounds they found the bones of men who stood eight feet tall—a race of giants, as the Nephilim of old, whose stature exceeded all men living in the present age. And the people marveled, for who were these giants, and what nation had produced them, and by what covenant had they been given such strength? The earth kept their secret, but their bones testified that the land of Kain-tuck-ee had known greatness from the very beginning.

5And the Shawnee hunted in the valleys, and the Cherokee hunted in the high places, and between the nations the land served as a common ground—a place where warriors, weary from battle, would retire to heal their wounds and hunt the abundant game. For the deer were as numberless as the stars, and the buffalo carved great roads through the canebrakes, and the wild turkey called from every hollow.

6No single nation claimed dominion over Kain-tuck-ee, for it was understood among the peoples that this was sacred ground—a hunting paradise, a place of rest between conflicts, a land held in trust for all who would come after.

7And the springs of Bath County bubbled forth from the earth, carrying minerals and medicines from deep within the stone. The natives knew these waters, and they came to bathe in them, and the waters healed their ailments. For the Lord had placed these springs as a covenant with the land itself—that whosoever would drink and bathe therein would find restoration.

8And the Licking River wound through the country like a serpent of blessing, watering the bottomlands, feeding the cane and the sycamore, providing passage for those who moved by water. Along its banks the Adena had buried their chieftains in log tombs, wrapped in the skins of animals, adorned with copper ornaments brought from the great lakes to the north.

9Thus was the land prepared, from the foundation of the world, for the people who would come to possess it. As a field is prepared before the seed, as a house is built before the family enters—so was Kain-tuck-ee made ready, through millennia of patient cultivation by the hand of the Almighty.

10And the Lord said unto the land: Be still. For I shall send my people unto thee in the fullness of time. They shall come from across the great water, and they shall be tested in the wilderness, and those who endure shall inherit thy abundance.

11For as it is written: the earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof. And He setteth the boundaries of nations, and He appointeth the times of their habitation, that they might seek after Him and find Him, though He be not far from any one of them.

12And so the land waited. The mounds stood silent. The springs flowed. The deer multiplied. And the spirit of the Creator brooded over Kain-tuck-ee like a hen over her eggs, waiting for the appointed hour.

II. The Book of the Wilderness

Daniel Boone and the Opening of Kain-tuck-ee
"And the Lord raised up a man from among the people,
a hunter and a pathfinder, to go before them into the land of promise."

1Now in the year of our Lord seventeen hundred and sixty-nine, on the seventh day of the sixth month, there arose a man named Daniel, of the house of Boone, born in the colony of Pennsylvania and raised in the Yadkin Valley of Carolina. And Daniel first beheld the wilderness of Kain-tuck-ee from a high ridge, and his heart was filled with wonder.

2For Daniel was a long hunter—one of those men who went forth from their settlements for months and years at a time, carrying only their long rifles, their powder horns, and their knowledge of the forest. They were as the patriarchs of old, wandering in lands not yet their own, dwelling in caves and beneath the sheltering rock.

3And the long rifle was unto them as the staff of Moses—by it they fed their families, by it they defended their lives, and by it they measured their worth among men. For a man who could shoot true at two hundred paces was a man to be trusted in all things.

4And Daniel spent two years in the wilderness, hunting and trapping, learning the ways of the land. He and his companion were taken captive by the Shawnee, but they escaped while their captors slept. For the Lord preserved Daniel, as He preserved the three young men in the furnace of Babylon, because he was appointed for a purpose.

5And the Cherokee chief, Dragging Canoe, spoke a prophecy over the land, saying: "There is a dark cloud over that country. It shall be a dark and bloody ground." And his words were true, for the land of promise is never given without blood. As Abel's blood cried from the ground, so would the blood of settlers and warriors alike water the soil of Kain-tuck-ee before it would yield its harvest.

6In the year seventeen hundred and seventy-five, Daniel was hired by the Henderson Company to blaze a trail from the settlements of Virginia through the Cumberland Gap and into the heart of the country. And this trail was called the Wilderness Road, and it was as the parting of the Red Sea—a passage through the mountains, where the walls of stone rose up on either side, and the way was narrow and perilous.

7And Daniel took with him thirty axe-men, and they cut the road through cane and brush and timber, mile by mile, day by day, until they came to the banks of the Kentucky River. And there, in the broad bottomland beneath the palisades, Daniel built a fort and called it Boonesborough.

8But hear this well, ye children of the mountains: the men went first, and the men went alone. For the land was not yet safe for women and children. The Shawnee raided by night, and the arrows flew in the darkness, and no man slept without his rifle at his side. The forts were as the camps of Israel in the desert—places of vigilance, of constant readiness, of prayer without ceasing.

9And the men bore this burden willingly. For they understood that before a family can be planted, the ground must be cleared. Before a garden can flourish, the stones must be pulled and the serpents driven out. This was the covenant of the frontier: that the strong shall go before the weak, and the armed shall make safe the way for the unarmed.

10And in August of that same year, seventeen hundred and seventy-five, Daniel brought his wife Rebecca and their daughter through the wilderness to Boonesborough. Rebecca was among the first white women to set foot in Kain-tuck-ee. And this was the sign that the land was being redeemed—for where women dwell, there civilization taketh root, and there children are born, and there the generations begin to multiply.

11But the cost was great. Daniel's own son, James, was killed by a war party of Cherokee, Delaware, and Shawnee as the family trekked through the wilderness. The boy was tortured before he died. And Daniel bore this grief as Abraham bore the binding of Isaac—knowing that the covenant requires sacrifice, and that the promised land is purchased with the blood of one's own.

12And the stations multiplied across the land. Boone's Station, Harrod's Fort, Logan's Station, Bryan's Station—each a cluster of cabins sharing a common wall, facing outward against the darkness. Like the camps of the twelve tribes, each station had its own patriarch, its own riflemen, its own stories told by firelight.

13And in the year seventeen hundred and seventy-eight came the great siege of Boonesborough, when the Shawnee and their British allies surrounded the fort for ten days. And Daniel led the defense, and the women melted pewter for bullets, and the children carried water from the spring under fire. And the fort did not fall.

14Let it be recorded: the frontier was not conquered by armies or by governments. It was conquered by families—by fathers who went first into danger, by mothers who followed with courage equal to their husbands', by children who grew up knowing that every blessing is earned through toil and vigilance.

15And this is the first lesson of the Appalachian people: Be still and know that I am God. Be courageous. Go before your family into the wilderness. Make the way safe. And then bring them forth into the abundance that the Lord hath prepared.

III. The Book of Settlement

The Forts and the Bringing of Women
"And when the land was made safe, the men sent word back across the mountains:
Come. Bring the women. Bring the children. The time of planting is at hand."

1And after the forts were built and the worst of the raiding had passed, there began a great migration. Tens of thousands of souls crossed the Cumberland Gap on the Wilderness Road, carrying what they could upon their backs and in their wagons. They came from Virginia and Carolina, from Pennsylvania and Maryland, seeking land and liberty.

2And among them came the families who would settle in the eastern highlands—the foothills where the Bluegrass meets the mountains, where the soil is rich and red, and the springs bubble up from the limestone below. These were the Appalachian people, and they were a people apart.

3For they did not seek the flat and easy land of the Bluegrass proper. They sought the hills and the hollows, the places where a man could carve out his own kingdom, answerable to no lord but God above. They were Scots-Irish and English and German, hardened by generations of border warfare in the old country, and they brought with them a fierceness of independence that would define their descendants for centuries to come.

4And the pattern of settlement was thus: first the man came alone, or with his brothers, and they cleared a patch of ground and built a cabin. They notched the logs at the corners—saddle notch or half-dovetail—and raised the walls in a day with the help of neighbors. The chimney they built of creek stones and red clay, and if the throat was not right, the smoke would fill the house and drive the children crying into the yard.

5And when the cabin stood and the first crop was in, then the man sent word back to his father's house: The land is good. Send my wife. Send my betrothed. The time of homemaking is begun.

6And the women came. And they brought with them the arts of civilization: the loom and the spinning wheel, the recipes for soap and candle, the knowledge of herbs and healing, the songs and the stories that would be told to children for generations hence. The men had conquered the wilderness; the women would tame it into a home.

7And in every settlement the same covenant held: the strong protect the weak. The armed make way for the unarmed. The father goes before; the mother follows after; the children inherit what both have built. This is the natural order, and this is the order of the Appalachian people from the days of the forts unto this present hour.

IV. The Book of Bath County

The Medicinal Springs and the Tobacco Fields
"And the Lord caused healing waters to spring forth from the deep places of the earth,
and He called the land Bath, for therein men would be made clean."

1In the year eighteen hundred and eleven, the fifty-sixth county was carved from Montgomery, and it was given the name Bath, for the great number of medicinal springs which abounded within its borders. Its seat was called Owingsville, and it sat at the boundary between two worlds: the rolling Bluegrass to the northwest and the rugged hills to the south and east.

2And the county lay upon the Licking River, that same river which had watered the ancient mound-builders. And the name Licking is older than any white man's memory—for in the age before memory, when the great beasts still walked the earth, the creatures came to this river to lick the salt from the banks, and the name of the river preserved that ancient visitation. The dinosaurs and the mastodons had knelt at these same waters; the mounds of the Adena rose above the same salt-licked banks. And the Richards tobacco farm would one day sit upon this same storied ground. And its tributaries ran through hollows where the poplar grew tall and the ginseng hid beneath the shade. The soil was dark and fertile in the bottomlands, and in the uplands the tobacco grew strong and broad-leafed beneath the Kentucky sun.

3Now tobacco was the golden leaf, the cash crop of the Appalachian farmer. For a man might grow corn and beans and hogs for his own table, but tobacco he grew for the market. And the cultivation of tobacco was an art passed from father to son: the setting of the tiny plants in spring, the suckering and topping in summer, the cutting and hanging in the barns in autumn, and the stripping and grading through the long winter months.

4And whosoever mastered the tobacco knew the rhythm of the land and the seasons, knew patience and labor, knew the uncertainty of weather and the mercy of God upon whom all harvests depend. For tobacco is a demanding master, and it teacheth a man that he reapeth only what he soweth, and that the slothful hand maketh poverty, but the diligent hand maketh rich.

5And in this county, among these hills and springs and tobacco fields, the house of Richards put down its roots. For they were among those families who settled the eastern highlands, who built their cabins along the creeks, who cleared the steep ground with axe and mattock, and who lived by the labor of their own hands.

6And the Richards family kept the old ways. They dressed their own hogs in November, salting the meat with black pepper to keep the flies away. They made their soap from ashes and lard. They planted by the signs of the moon, as their grandmothers had taught them. They drew their water from wells and heated their homes with fireplaces built of creek stone.

7And Madge Atchison owned the tobacco farm in Owingsville—that dark-soiled bottomland where the burley grew thick and golden. And Madge married John Adair Richards Sr, who was born in the year nineteen hundred and one in Owingsville. And thus the land of the Atchisons was joined to the house of Richards through the covenant of marriage, and the farm became the patrimony of both lines.

8And the courthouse at Owingsville was destroyed by fire in the year eighteen hundred and sixty-four, set ablaze by Union troops during the great war between the states. And the records of the county were consumed, and much that was written was lost. But that which is written in the hearts of men cannot be burned, and the stories survived in the telling.

9For it is written: Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away. And the words passed from mother to daughter, from father to son, carried in the memory like water in a clay vessel—precious because the vessel is fragile, and one generation's silence can shatter it forever.

V. The Book of Foxfire

The Knowledge of the Mountain People
"Daily our grandparents are moving out of our lives, taking with them, irreparably,
the kind of information contained in this book. They are taking it,
not because they want to, but because they think we don't care."
— Eliot Wigginton

1Hear now the knowledge of the mountain people, for it is a treasure beyond gold and silver. This knowledge was not learned in schools or read in books. It was earned through years of trial and error, passed by word of mouth and by demonstration, from grandmother to granddaughter, from old man to young boy, in the doing of daily work.

2And in the year nineteen hundred and sixty-six, in Rabun Gap, Georgia, deep in the Appalachian country, a young teacher named Wigginton came unto the mountain school. And he found his students restless and bored, for they were being taught from books about places they had never seen, while the living wisdom of their own grandparents was dying unrecorded all around them.

3And Wigginton said unto his students: "How would you like to throw away the text and start a magazine?" And that is how Foxfire began—a project to collect and preserve the knowledge of the Appalachian elders before it passed beyond recall.

4And the students went forth into the mountains with their tape recorders and their cameras, and they sat at the feet of the old ones, and they listened. And what they recorded was nothing less than the complete science of self-sufficiency—the total knowledge required for a family to sustain itself upon the land with nothing but their own hands and the grace of God.

The Arts of Building

The Log Cabin: Select your timber in winter when the sap is down. White oak for the sills, poplar or chestnut for the walls. Notch the corners with a saddle notch for a dwelling, or a half-dovetail if you want it to stand two hundred years. Chink between the logs with red clay and moss. Build your chimney of creek stones laid in clay mortar, and mind the throat—get it wrong and you'll smoke your family out like possums.

The Chimney: Set your foundation deep and firm in the ground. Use creek rocks and red clay mortar up past the throat. Above that, wooden slats about four inches wide laid on log-cabin fashion. Many a family woke to find their chimney flat in the bean patch because the foundation was not set deep enough.

The Arts of Sustenance

Hog Dressing: Kill your hogs when the weather turns cold in November. Salt the meat that same day with black pepper mixed in to keep the flies away. Hang it in the smokehouse on withes strung between the rafters. If the flies come, burn red pepper in an iron oven beneath the meat to drive them off.

Souse Meat: Take the hog's head and soak it overnight until the blood is drawn clean. Grind it on the sausage grinder. Take the juice it was cooked in, add sage and black pepper and red pepper, stir it fine, put it in canning jars and seal. Open in the winter. There is no finer eating.

Preserving: Dry your leather britches beans and fruits enough to last all winter. Kill a beef and a sheep and two or three hogs for the cold months. When mother ran out of coffee, she parched chestnuts and ground them on her coffee mill to make do.

The Arts of Making

White Oak Splits: Rive the white oak into thin strips for basket-making and hamper-weaving. The bark, split off green, serves for bottoming chairs. Withes of young saplings serve as rope for hanging meat in the smokehouse.

Soapmaking: Save your ashes from the fireplace all winter. Leach them with water to make lye. Combine the lye with rendered fat from hog-killing. Boil until it sets. This is your soap for the year—for washing clothes, for washing bodies, for scrubbing floors.

Spinning and Weaving: Shear the sheep in spring. Wash the wool and spin it into thread on the spinning wheel. Weave it on the loom to make blankets and cloth. Mother knit the stockings and socks. Father wore homemade britches of woven wool. Nothing was purchased that could be made by hand.

The Arts of the Earth

Planting by the Signs: Plant root crops in the dark of the moon. Plant above-ground crops in the light of the moon. Set fence posts in the dark of the moon lest they heave out of the ground. Kill hogs when the signs are in the feet, never in the head. The old ones knew these things, and their gardens prospered.

Weather Signs: When the smoke hangs low, rain is coming. When the leaves turn up their silver undersides, a storm approaches. When the katydids sing early, expect an early frost. The mountain people read the sky like a book, for their lives depended upon it.

Home Remedies and Faith Healing: For a burn, apply the inner bark of a linden tree. For croup, render the fat of a polecat. For a wound that will not close, bind it with cobwebs and the inside of an egg. And always pray over the sick, for the prayer of faith shall save them.

The Arts of Spirit

Moonshining: The making of corn liquor was called a fine art in the mountains, not because it was lawful, but because it required the mastery of fire and water and grain—the transformation of raw material into something of value. The copper still was as the alchemist's vessel, and the man who ran it understood chemistry by instinct and tradition.

Hunting: The hunter fed his family and knew the ways of every creature. He dressed and cooked the wild animals—deer, bear, turkey, squirrel, possum. He told hunting tales by the fire, and in those tales were encoded the lessons of patience, cunning, and respect for the natural world.

5And Aunt Arie, who lived alone in her log cabin with no running water and only a single fireplace for heat, spoke unto the young people who visited her, saying: "Don't never do a thing in this world that you don't want your poppy and mommy to know. And then, when the last roll's called, you'll be ready to go where you're gonna spend eternity out yonder. Remember that."

6And the old woman who could neither read nor write set down her memories in pencil on notebook paper, and she wrote: "My daddy raised the stuff we lived on. He growed the corn to make our bread. He growed the cane to make our syrup. He killed enough meat to last all winter. He didn't have much money for anything. We just had our biscuit for Sunday morning."

7And these words are as scripture unto the Appalachian people. For they testify to a way of life that was complete in itself—a life in which every need was met by the labor of one's own hands, in which every skill was sacred, and in which the family was the nation, and the hearth was the temple, and the father's voice was the law.

8Let it be written and let it be remembered: The Foxfire Journals are the Leviticus of the Appalachian people. They contain the rituals and the practices, the clean and the unclean, the proper times and the proper methods. Whosoever keepeth this knowledge keepeth the inheritance of the mountains.

VI. The Book of the Law

Fiduciary Duty and the Covenant of Trust
"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and thy neighbor as thyself.
Upon these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets."

1And the Lord gave unto His people not only the knowledge of the hands but the knowledge of the heart. For what profiteth a man if he can build a cabin and dress a hog, but hath not faithfulness in his dealings with his neighbor? Therefore, hear the law of trust, which is called in the tongue of lawyers fiduciary duty.

2A fiduciary is one who standeth in a position of trust over another. It is the father over the child, the steward over the master's flock, the lawyer over the client, the officer over the soldier, the elder over the congregation. And whosoever accepteth such a position taketh upon himself a sacred obligation: to act not for his own benefit, but for the benefit of the one who hath placed trust in him.

3And the law of fiduciary duty hath two pillars, even as the temple of Solomon had two pillars at its gates:

The First Pillar: The Duty of Loyalty Thou shalt act without personal economic conflict. Thou shalt not serve two masters.
Thou shalt place the interests of thy charge above thine own interests in all things.
Thou shalt not profit from thy position of trust at the expense of the one who trusteth thee.
The Second Pillar: The Duty of Care Thou shalt act with the utmost good faith, diligence, and prudence.
Thou shalt exercise such care as a wise and conscientious man would exercise
in the management of his own affairs. Thou shalt not be slothful in stewardship.

4And these two duties—loyalty and care—are the twin tablets of the covenant between servant and master, between steward and owner, between the man who is trusted and the one who trusts. They are the Decalogue of right dealing, and whosoever breaketh them bringeth ruin not only upon himself but upon all who depend upon him.

4bAnd hear now the origin of this duty, for it ariseth from necessity itself. Consider the slave who is sent by his master to the market to buy a horse. The slave standeth before the horse trader, and the trader maketh his offer, and the slave must answer. But the slave cannot practically ride back to his master for every back-and-forth of the negotiation—for the distance is great, and the opportunity fleeting, and the trader will not wait. Therefore, the slave must act as his master would act, must judge as his master would judge, must weigh cost and quality and necessity with the same mind as the one who sent him. This is fiduciary duty in its purest form: the obligation to stand in another's place and serve their interest as faithfully as if it were thine own.

4cAnd this same duty extendeth through the household. The wife who goeth to market while her husband laboreth in the field beareth fiduciary duty to the household purse. She negotiateth, she chooseth, she spendeth—and she must do so with the same prudence her husband would exercise, for she acteth in his stead and he cannot be summoned for every decision. And the child who is sent on an errand by his father beareth the same duty: to act as the father would act, to represent the household with the same care and loyalty as the head of that household would bring.

4dThis is why fiduciary duty is not merely a law for bankers and lawyers. It is the law of the family—the law of every person who acteth on behalf of another. It is the law that maketh civilization possible, for without it no man could send another in his stead, and every transaction would require the physical presence of the principal. Fiduciary duty is the trust that alloweth one man's reach to extend beyond his own two arms.

5Consider now how this law is woven through all scripture. Was not Joseph a fiduciary unto Pharaoh? Did he not steward the granaries and agricultural reserves of Egypt with loyalty and care, setting aside the surplus in years of plenty against the years of famine? And was he not elevated precisely because Pharaoh recognized in him a man who could be trusted with much, having proven faithful in little?

6And did not the Lord speak through the parable of the talents? The servant who was given five and returned ten was called good and faithful. The servant who was given one and buried it in the ground was cast into the outer darkness. For stewardship is not passive. It requireth action, risk, diligence—the active multiplication of that which is entrusted.

7And the parable of the unjust steward teacheth this: even a wicked man, when he perceiveth that his position of trust is ending, will use his remaining authority shrewdly to secure his future. How much more ought the righteous man to steward well while the day of stewardship lasteth?

8And the parable of the ten virgins teacheth this: keep thy wick trimmed and thy oil replenished. For the day of accounting cometh without warning, and only those who have maintained their readiness shall be received. This is fiduciary duty in its spiritual form—the constant preparation for the moment when one is called to give an account.

9Now hear the application of this law to the house of Richards: John Adair Richards Sr, the patriarch, was a lawyer in Bath County, Kentucky. And in his practice of the law he understood, more deeply than most men of his generation, that the entire edifice of civilization resteth upon trust. Remove trust, and the courts collapse. Remove trust, and commerce ceaseth. Remove trust, and the family itself disintegrateth into warring factions.

10And John Adair did his diligence upon jury members, for he knew that justice dependeth not merely upon the law as written but upon the character of those who administer it. A jury of faithful men rendereth a righteous verdict; a jury of faithless men perverteth justice regardless of the evidence.

11And John Adair emphasized fiduciary duty as the core component of legal precedent—teaching that all law, at its root, is the codification of trust. The contract is trust made enforceable. The will is trust extended beyond death. The corporation is trust multiplied among many parties. Remove the fiduciary principle, and all of law becometh mere force.

12And this teaching passeth down through the house of Richards as their particular commandment, their distinguishing mark among the families of the earth: Be faithful in thy trust. Steward well what is given thee. Act always as one who shall give account. For this is the servitude that pleaseth the Lord—not the servitude of the slave who feareth the lash, but the servitude of the steward who loveth the master's household as his own.

VII. The Book of John Adair

The Lawyer and the Tobacco Inheritance
"A good man leaveth an inheritance to his children's children,
and the wealth of the sinner is laid up for the just."
— Proverbs 13:22

1Now these are the generations of the house of Richards in Bath County, Kentucky. Madge Atchison owned the tobacco farm in Owingsville, on the Licking River—that ancient waterway where dinosaurs came to lick salt in ages beyond reckoning, where the dark-soiled bottomland along the creek grew burley thick and golden in the August sun. And the farm was the patrimony of the Atchison line, held by Madge as her own inheritance.

2And Madge Atchison, born in the year nineteen hundred and seven in Owingsville, married John Adair Richards Sr, who was born in the year nineteen hundred and one in that same town. And through this union the tobacco land of the Atchisons was joined to the house of Richards. John Adair was a lawyer, called to the bar and to the gate of justice. And together they built their household upon two pillars: the land and the law.

3And John Adair and Madge begot six children: John, Betty Ruth, Cookie, Morgan, Jeff, and Laurence. And the house was full, and the farm sustained them, and the children grew up in the rhythm of the tobacco seasons—setting plants in spring, suckering in summer, cutting in autumn, stripping through winter.

4And John Adair wrote the history of Bath County. Mark this well, children of Richards: he wrote it down. For the first commandment of legacy is this: that which is not written shall be forgotten. The spoken word lasteth one generation; the written word lasteth a thousand. And John Adair set down the stories of the county—the families, the feuds, the floods, the founding—and in so doing he preserved the memory of his people against the erosion of time.

5And this history of Bath County is the foundational text of the house of Richards. It is the record of who begot whom, of what happened and when, of the character of the people and the nature of the land. It containeth the story of Daniel Boone's passage through the country, of the ancient mounds, of the settlement and the building, of the wars and the peace that followed.

6And John Adair understood what the prophet understood: that a people without a written history is a people without a future. For how shall the young man know who he is, if he knoweth not who his fathers were? How shall he have courage, if he knoweth not the courage of his ancestors? How shall he stand in the day of trial, if he hath not heard the stories of those who stood before him?

7And so John Adair passed down three things to the house of Richards: the land, which came through Madge Atchison and is the body of the inheritance; the history, which he himself wrote and is the soul of the inheritance; and the law of fiduciary duty, which is the spirit of the inheritance—the animating principle that bindeth the generations together in mutual obligation and trust.

The Line of Richards in Bath County:

Madge Atchison owned the tobacco farm in Owingsville, and the land was good.

VIII. The Book of Jeff

The JAG, the Desert, and the Prosecution of Wickedness
"He hath showed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the Lord require of thee,
but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?"
— Micah 6:8

1And John Adair and Madge Richards begot Jeff E Richards, born on the twenty-fourth day of January in the year nineteen hundred and thirty, in Bath County, Kentucky. And Jeff grew up in the shadow of the law, learning from his father the principles of duty, trust, and justice. And as his father John Adair before him, Jeff studied law at the University of Kentucky—that same institution where his father had earned his degree. For the son followed in the father's footsteps, and the tradition of the law was passed not only by word but by the shared discipline of the same halls of learning.

2And when the call to war came, Jeff answered it, for the sons of the mountains have always answered the call to arms. He became a JAG—a Judge Advocate General—in the Korean War, serving his country not with rifle alone but with the instrument of military law.

2And after the war, the Lord led Jeff out of the green hills of Kentucky and into the desert. For as Moses was led from Egypt into the wilderness, so was Jeff led from the tobacco country into the arid land of Yuma, Arizona. 3And in Yuma, Jeff married Janice B. Chute, who was born on the third day of September in the year nineteen hundred and thirty-two, in Beverly, Essex County, Massachusetts. Let that number be written and remembered: twenty-one of twenty-one. For this was not cruelty but justice—the measured, impartial, unflinching application of the law against those who had committed the most grievous offenses against their neighbors.

Where gambling reigneth, the fathers of the people are impoverished. IX. The Book of Paul

The Technologist and the Chariots of Iron
"And the Lord was with Judah; and he drave out the inhabitants of the mountain;
but could not drive out the inhabitants of the valley,
because they had chariots of iron."
— Judges 1:19

1And Jeff and Janice Chute Richards begot three children in Yuma, Arizona: Charlotte Richards, Barbara Ruth Richards, and Paul Jefferson Richards, born in November of the year nineteen hundred and fifty-six. And Paul grew up in the desert country, alongside his sisters Charlotte and Barbara Ruth, the grandson of John Adair and Madge Atchison of the Appalachian hills, the son of the prosecutor, heir to the tradition of law and duty. But the Lord had appointed for Paul a different calling: he would not wield the sword of the law but the instrument of technology—the new tool by which nations would rise and fall in the modern age.

2And there was in those days a man named Ross Perot, who had founded a company called Electronic Data Systems in the year nineteen hundred and sixty-two. Perot was a son of the Naval Academy, a former salesman of IBM, who had seen with his own eyes how inefficiently the great institutions managed their costly machines. And Perot said: I shall build a company that manageth these systems for them, and I shall hire men of discipline and character to do this work.

3And Paul Richards followed Perot to Detroit, Michigan—that great city of iron and industry, where the chariots of the modern age were forged. For EDS was acquired by General Motors in the year nineteen hundred and eighty-four, and the work of managing the information systems of the world's largest manufacturer drew men of talent from across the nation.

4And in Detroit, Paul led the movement from procedural technology to object-oriented technology. Now hear this, for it is significant: procedural technology is as the old law—a rigid sequence of steps, followed without variation, in which each instruction must be executed in its appointed order. But object-oriented technology is as the new covenant—a living system in which each component knoweth its own nature and its own responsibilities, and acteth according to its kind.

5The shift from procedural to object-oriented was as the shift from the letter of the law to the spirit of the law. It was as the shift from slavery to stewardship—for in the old way, the machine did only what it was told, step by step, like a slave following orders. But in the new way, the machine was given understanding of its own role, and it acted with a kind of intelligence, responding to circumstances as a faithful steward respondeth to the needs of the household.

6And Paul was the first to hire women and Black people into management positions within his sphere of influence. Mark this: not merely to employ them, but to elevate them—to place them in positions of authority and trust. For Paul understood what his grandfather John Adair had taught through the law: that stewardship is not the privilege of one kind of person, but the calling of any person who demonstrateth faithfulness. The parable of the talents maketh no distinction of sex or color; it rewardeth only the one who multiplieth what is given.

7And Paul also led the Artificial Intelligence team at EDS—that vanguard of minds applying the new science of machine learning to the hardest problems of industry. And Paul wielded three instruments of this science: Supervised Learning, whereby the machine is taught by labeled example, learning from experience as a student learneth from a master; Unsupervised Learning, whereby the machine discovereth hidden patterns within the data, as a tracker readeth the forest floor without being told what to seek; and Reinforcement Learning, whereby the machine learneth through trial and consequence, earning reward for right action and bearing penalty for wrong, as a man is shaped by the discipline of life itself.

8And Paul applied these three to a single purpose: to make every product and every service better, cheaper, or faster. For this was the commandment of industry—the fiduciary duty of the engineer and the technologist to the enterprise they serve. Waste is a sin against the steward's calling. Inefficiency is a betrayal of the trust placed in the craftsman. And Paul drove out waste and inefficiency with the same relentlessness that his father Jeff had driven out vice in Yuma.

9And Paul worked upon the chariots of iron—the aircraft carriers that sailed the seas, the tanks that rolled across the battlefields, the jets that ruled the sky. For in the modern age, the chariot of iron is not a cart with wheels but a vessel of terrible complexity, and the management of its design and manufacture requireth a technology called Product Lifecycle Management.

10And Paul mastered the systems called Teamcenter and Windchill—great software architectures that tracked every bolt, every weld, every drawing, every change, from the first sketch upon the engineer's table to the final rivet driven on the factory floor. These systems were the scribes of industry, recording the lineage of every component as the Bible recordeth the lineage of every patriarch.

11And at the summit of his career, Paul worked for NASA at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, California—that temple of engineering where mankind's farthest-reaching instruments are conceived and commanded. And Paul worked on the rockets—those pillars of fire and thunder that lifted mankind from the dust of the earth into the heavens above. For if the frontier of Daniel Boone was the Cumberland Gap, the frontier of Paul's generation was the void between the stars. And the same courage that drove the long hunters into the unknown drove the engineers who built the vessels to carry men to the moon and beyond.

12And Paul's career spanned the entire arc of the technological age: from EDS in the automotive industry, to the Department of Defense, to the exploration of space. He had led the AI team thirty years before the world knew what AI was. He had seen the birth of the information age and had helped to shape its architecture. He was as Bezalel, whom the Lord filled with the Spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship.

13And Paul married Carole Hursley, born in the year nineteen hundred and sixty in Michigan—a daughter of the Hursley line, whose great-grandfather Frank McColl Hursley Sr and his wife Doris Hursley created the soap opera General Hospital. And Carole's father, Frank McColl Hursley Jr, earned a Bronze Star in World War II and married Lois Theisen, daughter of Leona McConologue—an Irish immigrant. And Paul and Carole raised their children on Bronco Road, in Clarkston outside of Detroit, near the waters where boats were kept and children grew up in the rhythm of the seasons. Their son Jefferson and their daughter Kelle Ann grew up together in the boating lifestyle upon the Michigan waters. And Paul taught his children by example: by thirty-five years of consulting and building and solving, by the discipline of showing up and doing the work, by the quiet faithfulness of a man who multiplieth his talents without fanfare or glory.

14But in the year two thousand and eight, a great recession fell upon the land—a financial calamity that rotted the social fabric of the Midwest, eating away at the communities of Michigan as a plague eateth at the harvest. The city of Detroit, which had been the forge of the nation, shuddered under the weight of its own decline. And Paul, ever the faithful steward, looked upon the land and saw that it could no longer sustain his children's flourishing.

15And Paul took his children Jefferson and Kelle to California—to the land of greater opportunity, where the sun shineth abundantly and new industries rose from the earth like wheat after the rain. And this was as Jacob sending his sons into Egypt: not an abandonment of the homeland but a strategic migration toward abundance, with the full intention that the blessing of the new land would one day flow back to the family.

16And Carole, their mother, gave them up with unconditional love. For this is the highest expression of a mother's love—not to hold her children back for her own comfort, but to release them into the fullness of what God hath prepared for them. As Hannah gave Samuel to the temple, so Carole gave her children to the wide world, trusting that the love she had poured into them would sustain them wherever they went.

For Paul blessed his children to return to the Hursley family gatherings at the great holidays—Thanksgiving, Christmas, and the summer feasts—that they might keep their roots in the Michigan soil even as their branches spread westward. And Jefferson and Kelle did return, year after year, and they sat at the Hursley table and heard the old stories, and they carried the family with them wherever they went.

X. The Book of Jefferson

The Blue Moon Son and the New Covenant
"For I know the plans I have for you, declares the Lord,
plans to prosper you and not to harm you,
plans to give you hope and a future."
— Jeremiah 29:11

1And Paul and Carole Richards begot two children: Jefferson Franklin Richards and Kelle Ann Richards. Jefferson was born on a Blue Moon, on the thirtieth day of August, in the year nineteen hundred and ninety-three, in Commerce Township, Michigan. And a Blue Moon is a rare thing—the second full moon within a single month—and it is a sign that the child born beneath it is set apart for uncommon purposes.

2And Jefferson grew up on Bronco Road, in a suburb outside of Detroit, in the boating lifestyle upon the waters of Michigan. He learned adventure and responsibility upon the lake—tubing and wakeboarding, reading the currents and the weather, knowing that water, like life, will drown the careless and carry the prepared.

3And in Carl's Woods, the boy explored the wild places near his home, catching turtles and fishing and playing with his companions. He developed a love for animals and the outdoors—that same love which had driven Daniel Boone into the wilderness two hundred years before. For the spirit of the frontiersman passeth through the blood, and it cannot be extinguished by suburbs or by schools.

4And Jefferson had a close friendship with his cousin Sean Wilson, and together they experienced the camaraderie of kinship and explored different spiritual systems. And his family holidays were divided between two houses—the Hursleys and the Richards—and from each he drew different gifts. From the Hursleys he learned the art of story, for his great-great-grandfather Frank McColl Hursley Sr and his wife Doris Hursley created the soap opera General Hospital. From the Richards he learned the law and the land.

5And in his adolescence, his parents divorced, and this shook the foundations of his understanding of family. For when the pillars of the temple crack, the child within trembleth. But the Lord useth even brokenness to build character, and Jefferson learned from this pain the preciousness of covenant—that which is broken must be rebuilt stronger, or not at all.

6And the family moved to California, to the warm and easy land beside the sea. And Jefferson graduated from Poway High School, and the graduation was a turning point—a heightened sense of reality and self-discovery. And in his eleventh year of schooling, he received an internship with a neurosurgeon in Boston, and there he gained humility and a deeper appreciation for life. For to stand beside one who holdeth the brain of man in his hands is to understand how fearfully and wonderfully we are made.

7And Jefferson went unto UC Davis for Sustainable Agriculture and Statistics. And hear this well: Sustainable Agriculture. The great-grandson of the tobacco farmer, the heir of the Bath County soil, chose to study the very science of the land itself. For the blood remembereth what the mind hath forgotten, and the call of the earth is never silenced in the sons of the mountains.

8And while at Davis, Jefferson visited Kentucky and worked on his family's tobacco farm with his second cousin James, son of John Richards—who was himself a son of John Adair and Madge Atchison. For James was of the same house, shaped by the same Bath County soil, and together the two young men worked the land of their common ancestors. And there Jefferson learned about subsistence living—the very knowledge preserved in the Foxfire Journals. He experienced the challenges and rewards of rural life. He faced fears and anxieties, but also found peace and inspiration in the countryside. He wrote and received love letters, and he contemplated his future beneath the same sky that had sheltered Madge and John Adair before him.

9And after a hiking trip with his friend Neel, Jefferson received counsel: "Learn from your father. He has thirty-five years of experience in object-oriented technology, systems integration, and software architecture. Go and learn at his feet." And Jefferson heeded this counsel, as a wise son heareth his father's instruction.

10And Jefferson apprenticed with his father Paul, observing his consulting work and learning the craft of PLM. And on his own time, in the year two thousand and fourteen, there came unto Jefferson two dreams in the night—as dreams came unto Joseph in the pit, and unto Daniel in Babylon.

11The first dream was a vision of the suffering of Jesus upon the cross—not the sanitized image of stained glass but the raw and terrible weight of it: the crown of thorns pressed into the skull, the nails through the wrists, the slow suffocation, the cry of dereliction. And Jefferson awoke shaken, understanding for the first time the full cost of the covenant—that the highest love is not comfort but sacrifice, that the seed must fall into the ground and die before it can multiply.

12The second dream was of language itself. Jefferson saw, as though from a great height, that the words of the great poets and the words of the great constitutions were not merely arrangements of letters but living structures—that hidden within them was a sequence, a code, a double helix of meaning wound through every line. He dreamed that the DNA of poetry and the DNA of law were the same substance, that the tongue which spake We the People and the tongue which spake In the beginning was the Word were drawing from the same deep well. And he resolved that he would build a tool to extract this sequence—to hold language up to the light and reveal the pattern within it.

13And from this dream was born Cortext.io, later known unto the world as LinguaLint.com. And Jefferson built algorithms for Natural Language Processing—teaching the machine to read as a scholar readeth, to weigh words as a judge weigheth testimony, to find the living structure beneath the surface of human expression. And he spoke with his friend Neel about advancements in NLP, and together they worked on the beginnings of this vision. And Jefferson started a paper boy service, collecting fifty emails in three days, and built robots to gather news from AP, Reuters, and ESPN—for the dream required data, and data required diligence.

14And Jefferson returned to school at San Diego Mesa College, earned an Associate's in Mathematics, and there he and Neel co-founded Sensutec—pursuing innovations in natural language processing and market analysis. For the dream of Cortext.io demanded a vessel, and Sensutec was that vessel: a company built by two friends who believed that the structure of language could be made visible to machines. And then Jefferson went to the University of Arizona, where he studied mathematics and finance.

15And at the University of Arizona, Jefferson completed an actuarial internship at Nautilus, learning the insurance industry from within. And he founded the Risk Runners club—which was not merely a student organization but the seed of a formal actuarial program at the University of Arizona itself. For Jefferson gathered the students of mathematics and statistics and finance, and he showed them that the science of risk was a sacred discipline: that to measure uncertainty is to stand at the boundary between what is known and what is unknown, and to serve faithfully in that gap. And through the Risk Runners, a program took root that would outlast its founder. He worked at Red Fox Analytics, implementing architectural shifts and demonstrating technical expertise.

16And Jefferson took sabbatical and traveled, reconnecting with family and friends, gaining new perspectives. He met his niece Juliet, and she inspired him to focus on his health and personal well-being. He learned to appreciate legal wisdom by spending a summer shadowing his Grandfather Richards. He learned to appreciate peace by spending a year supporting his Grandmother Hursley. He learned to appreciate grace from national holidays with Gramma Richards.

17And on the thirtieth day of August, thirty years after his birth, another Blue Moon rose in the sky. And this was also the reunion of Carole's birthing day—the day on which she had entered Jefferson into the world. And Carole, his mother, brought Jefferson and her new husband Bill Allen to Sedona, Arizona—that red-rock cathedral in the desert where the earth riseth up like the walls of a temple and the sky burneth copper and gold at the setting of the sun. And together they beheld the Blue Moon as it rose over the ancient stone, and it was a sign and a confirmation: that the covenant which began at Jefferson's birth was renewed, and that the purposes of the Lord are not abandoned but fulfilled in the fullness of time. Mother and son, standing beneath the same rare moon that had marked his arrival thirty years before.

18And Jefferson looked upon the scattered records of his family—the photographs from Bath County, the History written by John Adair, the Foxfire Journals of the mountain people, the book of fiduciary duty—and he said within his heart: These are our scriptures. These are the texts of the house of Richards. I shall gather them together and make of them a Bible for my people, that our children's children may know who they are and from whence they came.

19For Jefferson understood the meta-model of greatness—the pattern woven through all of scripture, from Abraham to David: that parents must tell stories to their children about their ancestors. That the stories must be written down. That the family must pray together, focusing on the same purpose. That every man must be the best servant he can be to those around him. That opportunity cometh to the prepared. That he who is faithful in little shall be trusted with much.

XI. Exodus

The Multiplication and the Way Forward
"And ye shall teach them your children, speaking of them when thou sittest in thine house,
and when thou walkest by the way, when thou liest down, and when thou risest up.
And thou shalt write them upon the door posts of thine house, and upon thy gates."
— Deuteronomy 11:19-20

1Now hear the summation of this book, ye children of Richards, ye sons and daughters of the Appalachian mountains. For this is not merely a history—it is a covenant. It is not merely a record—it is a commandment. The commandment is this: Remember. Write it down. Tell the stories. And be faithful.

2For as Joseph was sold into slavery in Egypt, and yet by his faithfulness rose to steward all the granaries and agricultural reserves of Pharaoh—so may any child of this house, in whatever station of servitude they find themselves, rise by the same principle. The principle is not luck. The principle is not privilege. The principle is this: Be the best damn servant you can be to whomever is around you, beginning with your father, your brothers, your neighbors, and your family.

3And as Joseph interpreted the king's dream, saying "There shall come seven years of plenty and seven years of famine; therefore set aside grain in the years of abundance"—so must every generation understand the cycle of feast and famine. Sacrifice in the time of plenty. Set aside reserves. Produce more than you consume. For the famine cometh surely, and only the prepared shall survive it.

4And as Joseph's family multiplied in Egypt until Pharaoh grew fearful and pressed them into harder bondage—so must every family understand that prosperity bringeth its own dangers. When a family hath multiplied and pulled its resources together, the principalities of this world will take notice, and they will seek to make servants of the successful. This is the natural order of power, and it is not to be feared but anticipated.

5And as Moses arose from among the enslaved to lead his people forth—so must every generation produce its radical leader. Not radical in the sense of destruction, but radical in the sense of root—radix—one who returneth to the roots, who remembereth the covenant when all others have forgotten, who standeth before the powers of the world and saith: Let my people go.

6And the formula is this, and let it be inscribed upon the doorposts of every house of Richards:

The Formula of Greatness First: Pray to God. On your knees, in humility, regularly. Have faith. Be still. Do not fear.

Second: Know that you are chosen. Know that your family is chosen. Know that it will be well.

Third: Be a servant. The best servant you can be. To your father, your brothers, your neighbors.

Fourth: Pay attention. If you cannot pay the bills, pay attention. Be steadfast. Be ready.

Fifth: Prepare soil and seed before the rain. Practice, practice, practice—so that when God's reign cometh, you are ready to receive it.

Sixth: Recognize the opportunity. Interpret the dream. Remind the king to sacrifice. Step forward when others hesitate.

Seventh: Steward well what is given. Be faithful in little, and you shall be trusted with much.

Eighth: Write it down. Tell the stories. Keep the family Bible. For a people without written memory is a people without a future.

7And concerning the worship of God: worship not graven images. Worship not the phone, the screen, the algorithm, the bank account. These are the idols of the modern age. Worship the Creator—go inward, go outward, go upward. For God is the I AM: first person singular, first person plural, second person singular, third person singular. God is I, We, You, He, She, It. God is not They—not the faceless crowd, not the mob, not the collective stripped of personhood.

8And concerning the wisdom of the Chinese farmer: when the horse runneth away, say maybe. When the horses return with mares, say maybe. When thy son breaketh his leg upon the horse, say maybe. When the army passeth over thy son because of his broken leg, say maybe. For the wise man judgeth not the moment but waiteth upon the Lord, knowing that what seemeth cursed may be a blessing, and what seemeth blessed may be a trial.

8bAnd mark this well: the neighbors who cast his luck—crying fortunate and unfortunate at every turn—are as society itself, which casteth identity upon a man like a net. Society saith: thou art lucky, thou art cursed, thou art a winner, thou art a loser, thou art this title, thou art that station. And through its coercions and its conferred labels it seeketh to fix a man in place, to make of him a known quantity, to strip him of the maybe that is his birthright before God. But the farmer answereth not to the neighbors' verdict. He answereth only to the unfolding of the story, which no neighbor can read to its end. Therefore: receive no man's title as thy final word. Wear society's labels as a traveler weareth a borrowed coat—useful for the weather, but not thy skin.

9And concerning the Spartans: if all three hundred pray for the same thing, focusing on the same objective, then one among them shall receive the insight, shall raise his hand and say, "I have an idea. Let us bottleneck the enemy and fight where our strength is multiplied." But this happeneth not when each man prayeth for himself alone. Unified prayer produceth collective revelation. This is the mystery of the gathered family.

10And concerning the wicked: as Proverbs warneth, there will always be those who say, "Let us pool our resources. Let us have one purse between us." Run from these people. They speak the language of community but practice the art of theft. Their communism is not sharing but control. Guard thy purse. Guard thy independence. For the family that pooleth its resources with strangers loseth both its wealth and its sovereignty.

11And concerning David's administration: the end in mind is the royal court of Second Samuel. A family grown from a single cabin on the creek to an administration—each role filled, each responsibility defined, each person trusted in their office. The recorder, the scribe, the commander, the priest, the counselor. This is the mature form of the family: not one man bearing all burdens, but a court of faithful stewards, each serving in their appointed place.

12And it may not happen in thy generation. It may not happen in four generations. The point is to teach these things, to continue to have children, to maintain the rites and rituals and passages and holidays, and to trust that the Lord worketh across centuries, not merely across years. The oak that standeth in the square was planted by a man who never saw its shade. Plant anyway.
